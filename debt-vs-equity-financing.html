Debt vs. Equity Financing

How to choose the right capital structure for your business — ownership, cost, risk, and control.

Fred Medley·CEO, The Medley Group·7 min read·November 2026

When a company needs capital, one of its most important decisions is whether to borrow money or raise funds from investors.

Both approaches can support business growth. Both can also create significant financial consequences.

Debt financing allows a business to obtain capital through borrowing, while equity financing involves selling an ownership interest. The appropriate choice depends on the company's financial condition, growth strategy, risk tolerance, and long-term objectives.

Understanding debt financing

Debt financing involves borrowing capital under an agreement requiring repayment.

Common forms include commercial term loans, revolving credit facilities, equipment financing, asset-based loans, and certain subordinated financing arrangements. Debt providers may require collateral, guarantees, financial covenants, or other protections.

The primary advantage is that borrowing generally allows existing owners to retain their ownership interests. The primary disadvantage is that repayment obligations remain even when the business underperforms.

Understanding equity financing

Equity financing involves obtaining capital from investors in exchange for ownership interests or other equity rights.

Unlike conventional debt, equity generally does not require fixed monthly principal and interest payments. However, investors may receive a portion of future profits, proceeds from a business sale, and certain governance rights.

Equity financing can therefore provide flexibility while creating long-term ownership and control considerations.

Debt versus equity: key differences

Factor Debt financing Equity financing
Ownership Generally retained Typically diluted
Repayment Contractual obligation Generally no fixed repayment
Interest expense Usually applicable Not generally applicable
Collateral May be required Generally not required for the investment itself
Investor control Usually through covenants May include voting or approval rights
Financial risk Default and refinancing risk Dilution and investor-rights risk
Cost Interest, fees, and other terms Share of future enterprise value

Example: financing a $10 million business expansion

Consider an established company seeking $10 million to expand its operations. Management is evaluating two alternatives.

Option A: debt financing

The company borrows $10 million at a hypothetical fixed annual interest rate of 8%, amortized over seven years with monthly payments.

Total interest ≈ $3.09M · 84 equal monthly payments at 8%/12, no additional fees or prepayments

The company retains its existing ownership but must generate enough cash flow to support approximately $1.87 million in annual debt service.

The financing cost is significant, and the company assumes the risk of meeting its payment obligations regardless of operating performance.

Option B: equity financing

Instead of borrowing, the company raises $10 million from an investor. Assume the company has a negotiated pre-money equity valuation of $30 million. The new investor receives 25% of the post-investment ownership.

The company has no conventional loan payments associated with that investment, but existing shareholders now own 75% rather than 100%.

Which option is less expensive?

That depends on the company's future performance and the investment agreement.

Suppose the business eventually sells for an equity value of $80 million. If the new investor owns 25% of the company, that investor's ownership would represent $20 million of the sale proceeds, assuming no other relevant capital structure adjustments or preferential terms.

This illustrates why equity can be economically expensive when a business grows substantially.

However, the comparison is not as simple as placing $20 million of future equity proceeds against $3.09 million of loan interest. A rigorous analysis must also consider the repayment of loan principal, the timing of cash flows, taxes, future financing, investment returns, the time value of money, and the risk of business failure.

A reliable financing decision requires comparing complete financial scenarios — not isolated figures.

When debt financing may be preferable

Debt financing may be attractive when a company has stable earnings, predictable cash flow, sufficient borrowing capacity, and owners who want to retain control.

It can also be appropriate when the financed investment is expected to generate returns that comfortably exceed the full cost of borrowing. But borrowing too aggressively can create financial distress, even for profitable businesses.

When equity financing may be preferable

Equity financing may be attractive when a business is pursuing substantial growth, has limited collateral, or cannot reasonably support additional debt payments.

It can also be useful when management seeks a strategic partner with industry expertise, distribution capabilities, or other resources. However, business owners should evaluate the long-term cost of dilution and the governance rights granted to investors.

What about combining debt and equity?

Businesses do not necessarily have to choose one or the other. A blended capital structure can provide flexibility by combining the benefits of multiple financing sources.

For example, a $25 million acquisition might be financed through $15 million of senior debt, $5 million of preferred equity, and $5 million of sponsor common equity. Such a structure could reduce the senior debt burden compared with a heavily leveraged acquisition while allowing the sponsor to retain a meaningful ownership position.

Nevertheless, preferred equity may introduce significant economic and control obligations. Every financing layer must be evaluated in relation to the others.

Five factors that should drive the decision

  1. Cash flow: Can the company meet debt obligations under realistic and stressed operating conditions?
  2. Cost of capital: What is the complete economic cost of each financing alternative?
  3. Ownership: How much control and future economic value are owners willing to surrender?
  4. Financial flexibility: How will the financing affect future borrowing capacity and business decisions?
  5. Exit strategy: How will investors or lenders be repaid, refinanced, or otherwise receive their expected returns?

These considerations should guide financing decisions before the business commits to a particular capital source.
