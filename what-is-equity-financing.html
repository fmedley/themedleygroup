What Is Equity Financing?

How businesses raise capital by selling ownership — and the real tradeoffs of doing so.

Fred Medley·CEO, The Medley Group·6 min read·November 2026

Every growing business eventually faces an important financial question: where will the capital come from to support its next stage of growth?

Traditional business loans can provide financing, but they also create repayment obligations and may require collateral or personal guarantees.

For businesses pursuing significant expansion, acquisitions, new product development, or major capital investments, another option is equity financing.

Equity financing allows a business to raise capital by selling an ownership interest to investors.

Unlike a traditional loan, equity capital generally does not require scheduled principal and interest payments. However, this flexibility comes with an important tradeoff: existing owners may give up a portion of their ownership, future profits, or decision-making authority.

How equity financing works

When a company raises equity capital, investors contribute money in exchange for shares, membership interests, or other ownership securities.

The investors' potential returns are generally tied to the financial performance and value of the business. Depending on the investment agreement, investors may receive distributions, voting rights, preferred economic rights, or proceeds from a future sale.

Equity financing is not limited to startups. Established companies also use it to fund acquisitions, strengthen their balance sheets, finance expansion, and support ownership transitions.

Example: raising $10 million in equity capital

Assume a privately held company has an agreed pre-money equity valuation of $30 million and wants to raise $10 million from an outside investor.

Post-money valuation
$40M
$30M pre-money + $10M new investment · Existing owners 75% / New investor 25%

The new investor contributes $10 million and receives 25% of the company's post-investment equity. Existing owners retain 75%.

The company gains $10 million in new capital without creating a conventional loan repayment obligation. However, the existing owners now share the company's future economic value with the new investor.

Assumes a simple new-money equity issuance with no other dilution, preferred economic terms, or transaction costs.

Common sources of equity financing

Private equity firms invest in businesses with the objective of generating returns through operational improvement, expansion, acquisitions, or eventual sale. Some investments involve controlling ownership positions, while others involve minority interests.

Venture capital investors generally focus on companies with substantial growth potential, often in technology, healthcare, and emerging industries. Venture capital may suit companies pursuing rapid expansion, but investors often expect significant returns and defined liquidity opportunities.

Angel investors are individuals who invest their own capital in private businesses. They may provide financial resources, industry knowledge, and professional relationships.

Strategic investors are businesses or organizations that invest in another company to pursue commercial advantages, such as market expansion, technology development, distribution relationships, or joint ventures.

Preferred equity investors provide capital in exchange for negotiated priority rights. These arrangements can be particularly relevant in commercial real estate, acquisitions, and structured finance transactions.

Advantages of equity financing

Equity financing can reduce the need for scheduled debt payments, improve financial flexibility, and provide capital for projects that may not immediately generate sufficient cash flow to support traditional borrowing.

Some investors also contribute valuable expertise, business relationships, and strategic guidance. In addition, a stronger equity base may improve a company's ability to obtain future debt financing, although it does not guarantee lender approval.

Disadvantages of equity financing

The most significant disadvantage is ownership dilution. Selling equity means sharing future enterprise value with investors.

A business that grows substantially may ultimately transfer far more economic value to investors than it would have paid in interest on a conventional loan.

Equity financing can also introduce governance requirements, reporting obligations, investor approval rights, and restrictions on certain business decisions. Owners should carefully review the investment's economic and control provisions before proceeding.

Is equity financing regulated?

Yes. Selling ownership interests generally involves securities laws.

The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission explains that companies offering securities must either register the offering or qualify for an exemption. Private businesses commonly explore exemptions under Regulation D and other available securities-law provisions.

The SEC has also emphasized the risks associated with private placements, including illiquidity, limited disclosure, and the potential loss of invested capital.

Businesses raising equity should obtain appropriate securities-law advice and ensure that anyone receiving compensation for arranging an investment complies with applicable registration requirements.

When should a business consider equity financing?

Equity financing may be worth considering when a company has meaningful growth opportunities but insufficient cash flow to support additional borrowing.

It may also be appropriate when management wants to reduce leverage, fund a transformative acquisition, or bring in a strategic investment partner.

However, raising equity should not be viewed as automatically preferable to borrowing. The decision should account for the company's valuation, cost of capital, ownership objectives, governance requirements, and long-term growth strategy.

The Medley Group perspective

At The Medley Group, we believe equity financing should be evaluated as part of a comprehensive capital strategy.

The question is not simply how much capital a company can raise. It is how much ownership the company must surrender, what rights investors will receive, and whether the financing supports the company's long-term objectives.

Equity capital can accelerate growth. The challenge is ensuring that the value created justifies the ownership surrendered.
