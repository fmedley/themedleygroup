How to Prepare for $5M–$50M in Commercial Financing

What lenders and investors evaluate, which documents to prepare, and how to position your company for a successful financing process.

Fred Medley·CEO, The Medley Group·9 min read·November 2026

For established businesses pursuing acquisitions, major expansions, commercial real estate investments, or strategic growth initiatives, obtaining $5 million to $50 million in financing is fundamentally different from applying for a traditional small-business loan.

At this level, lenders and institutional capital providers evaluate more than revenue, collateral, and credit history. They examine the company's financial performance, management capabilities, cash flow sustainability, capital structure, industry exposure, and ability to execute its business strategy.

A company generating $30 million in annual revenue may struggle to obtain a $10 million credit facility, while a smaller business with strong recurring cash flow, quality collateral, and disciplined financial management may present a more attractive credit opportunity.

The difference often comes down to preparation.

Businesses that approach financing strategically are generally better positioned to present a credible transaction, address underwriting concerns, and negotiate appropriate financing terms.

1. Clearly define why your business needs capital

One of the first questions a lender or investor will ask is straightforward: What exactly will the money be used for?

A financing request should identify a specific business purpose and demonstrate how the proposed capital will support that objective. Common purposes include business acquisitions, commercial real estate purchases, construction and development, equipment investments, working capital, refinancing, and expansion into new markets.

However, a general statement such as "We need $15 million to grow" is rarely sufficient. A stronger request might explain that the company requires $15 million to acquire a regional competitor, integrate its operations, expand distribution capacity, and fund transaction-related working capital.

The company should be prepared to provide a detailed sources-and-uses schedule identifying the financing amount, equity contribution, transaction expenses, and anticipated allocation of funds.

Example: a $20 million acquisition financing request

Sources of capital Amount
Senior acquisition financing $12,000,000
Subordinated financing $3,000,000
Buyer equity contribution $5,000,000
Total sources $20,000,000
Uses of capital Amount
Business purchase price $18,000,000
Transaction expenses $750,000
Integration and working capital $1,250,000
Total uses $20,000,000

Illustrative transaction only. The financing structure is not a representation of current lender terms or an indication that a particular transaction would qualify.

This level of detail demonstrates that management has considered both the acquisition itself and the capital needed to operate the business after closing.

2. Prepare a comprehensive financial package

Financial reporting quality is one of the most important elements of a commercial financing request. Lenders must understand how the business generates revenue, converts earnings into cash, manages liabilities, and supports existing and proposed financial obligations.

For substantial financing transactions, companies should generally be prepared to provide:

  • Three years of historical financial statements, where available
  • Current interim financial statements
  • Business tax returns, as requested
  • Detailed schedules of existing debt obligations
  • Accounts receivable and payable aging reports
  • Historical and projected cash flow statements
  • Financial forecasts with clearly documented assumptions
  • Ownership and organizational information
  • Relevant collateral schedules and supporting valuations

The exact documentation requirements vary by lender, financing product, company size, and transaction complexity. A lender may require audited financial statements for certain transactions, while other financing sources may accept reviewed or internally prepared statements.

The important principle is consistency. Financial statements, tax filings, management reports, and financing projections should tell a coherent financial story. Material discrepancies should be reconciled and explained before a financing request is submitted.

3. Understand your company's debt capacity

One of the most common financing mistakes is assuming that strong revenue automatically translates into borrowing capacity. Revenue matters, but lenders place significant emphasis on cash flow and the company's ability to meet debt obligations.

A business with $50 million in annual revenue but weak operating margins may have less borrowing capacity than a company generating $20 million in revenue with consistent earnings and strong cash conversion.

Understanding debt-service coverage

A common underwriting measure is the debt-service coverage ratio (DSCR):

DSCR = Cash Flow Available for Debt Service ÷ Required Debt Service

Consider a hypothetical business generating $3 million in annual cash flow available for debt service, with $2 million in required annual principal and interest payments.

Illustrative debt-service coverage
1.50x
$3,000,000 ÷ $2,000,000 = 1.50x — for every $1.00 of required debt service, $1.50 of defined cash flow

This may indicate a meaningful cash flow cushion, but it does not automatically establish creditworthiness. Lenders define eligible cash flow differently. Some may adjust earnings for capital expenditures, distributions, working capital requirements, or other obligations.

Debt-service coverage requirements also vary by lender, industry, collateral, and financing structure. Companies should calculate their debt capacity using the methodology relevant to their proposed financing.

4. Develop realistic financial projections

Historical performance tells lenders where a business has been. Financial projections help explain where management expects the business to go — and whether its strategy is financially achievable.

For significant financing requests, management should consider developing a three- to five-year financial forecast, with greater detail in the first 12 to 24 months. A useful forecast should include revenue, operating expenses, earnings, working capital, capital expenditures, debt service, and projected cash balances.

Projections should also explain the assumptions driving expected growth. For example, a manufacturer projecting a 30% revenue increase should demonstrate how additional production capacity, customer demand, staffing, and working capital will support that growth.

Stress-test the financing request

A credible financial model should evaluate more than an optimistic operating scenario.

Scenario DSCR
Base case 1.50x
10% cash flow decline 1.35x
25% cash flow decline 1.13x

Assumes debt service remains unchanged. These scenarios are illustrative and do not establish lender approval thresholds.

This exercise can reveal how quickly a company's debt-service coverage deteriorates when operating performance weakens. A financing structure that appears manageable under ideal conditions may become difficult to support during a downturn.

5. Evaluate the right type of financing

Not every $5 million to $50 million financing request belongs with a traditional commercial bank. Different capital providers serve different purposes and have different underwriting requirements.

Financing source Potential application
Commercial banks Established businesses with qualifying cash flow and credit profiles
Asset-based lenders Businesses with eligible receivables, inventory, or other collateral
Private credit funds Transactions requiring customized or nontraditional debt structures
Mezzanine lenders Acquisitions, growth financing, and leveraged transactions
Commercial real estate lenders Property acquisition, refinancing, and development
Equity investors Growth initiatives requiring ownership capital
Specialty finance companies Equipment, receivables, or industry-specific financing

A business seeking $25 million to acquire a competitor may require a fundamentally different financing structure from a developer seeking $25 million for a construction project. The objective should be to match the financing source to the transaction's cash flow, collateral, risk characteristics, and long-term business strategy.

6. Strengthen your management and operational story

Institutional lenders and investors evaluate the people responsible for executing the business plan. A company may have attractive financial statements, but lenders also want confidence in its leadership team.

Management should be prepared to demonstrate relevant industry experience, financial discipline, operational capabilities, and a record of executing business initiatives.

For acquisition financing, lenders may examine management's experience integrating acquired companies. For expansion financing, they may evaluate whether the organization has the personnel, systems, and infrastructure necessary to support increased operating activity.

A well-prepared financing package should include concise executive biographies, an organizational chart, and a clear explanation of management responsibilities. Strong leadership does not eliminate financial risk, but it can improve the credibility of the proposed transaction.

7. Identify and address credit concerns before applying

A strong financing package does not hide weaknesses. It identifies material risks and explains how management intends to address them.

Common underwriting concerns include customer concentration, declining margins, excessive leverage, inconsistent financial reporting, weak collateral coverage, pending litigation, and dependence on a small number of executives.

For example, if 40% of a company's revenue comes from one customer, management should explain the relationship's contractual terms, historical stability, and contingency plans. Similarly, if earnings declined during the previous fiscal year, the financing package should distinguish temporary disruptions from structural business problems.

Federal banking supervisory guidance consistently identifies repayment capacity, collateral, loan structure, and credit controls as central elements of commercial credit underwriting. This reinforces an important principle: a compelling growth story does not replace a credible repayment strategy.

Research from the Federal Reserve Banks' Small Business Credit Survey illustrates why preparation and financing-source selection matter. Among surveyed employer firms that applied for financing:

Received all financing sought 42%

Received none 22%

These findings do not establish approval probabilities for $5 million to $50 million transactions — the survey covers businesses with fewer than 500 employees and includes many much smaller financing requests. Nevertheless, the research demonstrates that obtaining the full amount of requested financing is not automatic. For executives pursuing substantial transactions, this underscores the value of realistic capital planning and identifying suitable financing providers.

9. Evaluate the entire financing proposal — not just the interest rate

When reviewing financing proposals, borrowers sometimes focus too heavily on the quoted interest rate. However, two financing proposals with similar rates can produce very different financial outcomes.

Executives should evaluate the complete economic and contractual terms, including origination fees, amortization, maturity, collateral, guarantees, covenants, prepayment provisions, reporting obligations, and refinancing requirements.

For example, a five-year loan with a large balloon payment may create substantially different liquidity risks than a fully amortizing seven-year facility. A lower interest rate may also come with more restrictive covenants or additional collateral requirements. For floating-rate loans, borrowers should consider interest-rate sensitivity and whether hedging arrangements may be appropriate.

The objective is to understand the total cost, flexibility, and risk of the financing, rather than selecting a lender based solely on headline pricing.

10. Prepare a professional financing memorandum

For larger transactions, a well-organized financing memorandum can help management communicate the opportunity efficiently. This document should give prospective financing providers a clear understanding of the business, the proposed transaction, and the financial rationale.

A financing memorandum commonly includes an executive summary, company background, industry overview, historical financial performance, financing request, sources and uses, projected cash flow, repayment strategy, management team, and principal transaction risks. Supporting documents should be organized in a secure electronic data room with appropriate confidentiality and access controls.

A professional presentation cannot overcome a fundamentally weak transaction. However, it can reduce unnecessary delays, improve the quality of lender discussions, and help ensure that decision-makers receive consistent information.

11. Establish a realistic financing timeline

A $5 million to $50 million financing transaction may involve multiple stages of review: initial screening, financial analysis, preliminary terms, due diligence, credit approval, documentation, and closing. Complex transactions may also require appraisals, environmental reports, collateral examinations, legal opinions, or third-party financial due diligence.

There is no universal closing timeline. A straightforward transaction involving an existing banking relationship may progress differently from a leveraged acquisition involving multiple capital providers.

Companies should avoid committing to acquisition closing dates or major expenditures based solely on an informal indication of lender interest. A term sheet, preliminary discussion, or expression of interest is not necessarily a binding financing commitment.

12. Know when to engage a capital advisor

Businesses often begin the financing process by contacting their existing bank. That can be an appropriate first step, particularly when the bank understands the company's operating history and financial performance.

However, a company's financing requirements may extend beyond a single institution's lending appetite, credit limits, or product capabilities. An experienced capital advisor can help management evaluate the financing request, identify potential capital sources, compare structures, and coordinate the financing process.

This can be especially valuable for acquisitions, recapitalizations, complex commercial real estate projects, and transactions requiring multiple financing sources. The advisor's role should be clearly defined, with transparent compensation arrangements and appropriate regulatory compliance.

The objective is not simply to obtain a financing proposal. It is to help the company evaluate whether the proposed financing supports its business strategy and financial capacity.

Your commercial financing readiness checklist

Before initiating discussions with lenders or investors, use the following to evaluate your company's preparation:

  • Defined financing purpose and requested amount
  • Balanced sources-and-uses schedule
  • Historical and current financial statements
  • Existing debt and collateral schedules
  • Documented financial projections and assumptions
  • Debt-service and downside scenario analysis
  • Management biographies and ownership information
  • Identified material business and transaction risks
  • Prepared executive financing memorandum
  • Established transaction timeline and advisory team

Completing this checklist does not guarantee financing approval. It does, however, provide a practical starting point for a more disciplined financing process.

The Medley Group perspective: capital readiness is a strategic advantage

Securing $5 million to $50 million in commercial financing requires more than finding a lender willing to consider the request. It requires understanding the company's financial capacity, presenting a credible business strategy, identifying appropriate capital sources, and structuring financing around both immediate objectives and long-term sustainability.

For business owners and executive teams, the most effective financing process begins well before an application is submitted. At The Medley Group, we believe that capital decisions should be evaluated in the context of the entire business — whether a company is pursuing an acquisition, expanding operations, refinancing existing obligations, or exploring alternative capital structures.

The goal is not simply to secure capital. It is to secure capital that positions the business for its next stage of growth.
