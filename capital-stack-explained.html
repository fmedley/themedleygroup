Capital Structuring

The Capital Stack Explained

Understanding how commercial real estate and business transactions are financed — from senior debt to common equity.

Fred Medley·CEO, The Medley Group·6 min read·November 2026

When businesses pursue acquisitions, commercial real estate developments, major expansions, or recapitalizations, one of the most important decisions is how the transaction will be financed.

That decision involves more than securing a loan. It requires understanding the capital stack — the combination of debt and equity used to fund a transaction.

A properly structured capital stack can improve financial flexibility, manage risk, and help preserve ownership. A poorly structured one can create excessive debt obligations, restrict cash flow, or expose owners to unnecessary financial pressure.

What is a capital stack?

The capital stack represents the different sources of financing used in a transaction, organized according to their contractual priority and risk.

In commercial real estate, the four commonly discussed layers are senior debt, mezzanine debt, preferred equity, and common equity.

Illustrative capital stack — $20 million transaction
Common Equity$2M · 10%
Preferred Equity$2M · 10%
Mezzanine Debt$3M · 15%
Senior Debt$13M · 65%
Top = higher risk / lower priority · Bottom = highest priorityIllustrative only

The four layers of the capital stack

1. Senior debt: the foundation

Senior debt generally represents the largest financing component. It is commonly provided by banks, insurance companies, debt funds, or other institutional lenders.

In commercial real estate, senior debt is typically secured by a first-priority mortgage or deed of trust.

Because senior lenders generally hold the highest contractual priority among financing providers, they typically accept lower returns than subordinate capital providers.

However, senior financing also imposes requirements. Borrowers may need to satisfy loan-to-value ratios, debt-service coverage requirements, financial reporting covenants, and other underwriting conditions.

2. Mezzanine debt: bridging the financing gap

Mezzanine financing occupies a position between senior debt and equity.

It is often used when senior financing does not provide sufficient capital and the sponsor wants to avoid contributing additional common equity.

Mezzanine debt generally carries higher financing costs than senior debt because the lender accepts a subordinate position and greater risk.

In commercial real estate, mezzanine financing is frequently secured by a pledge of ownership interests in the property-owning entity rather than a direct mortgage on the property.

3. Preferred equity: capital with negotiated priority

Preferred equity allows investors to contribute capital in exchange for negotiated economic rights.

These rights may include priority distributions, preferred returns, redemption provisions, or additional protections.

Preferred equity is not automatically debt. Its treatment depends on the governing agreements and applicable law.

Unlike common equity investors, preferred equity investors generally receive certain distributions before common equity holders, subject to the transaction documents.

4. Common equity: ownership and residual returns

Common equity is the ownership capital contributed by the sponsor and other investors.

Common equity investors generally receive the remaining economic benefits after higher-priority obligations and distributions are satisfied.

They also bear substantial downside risk. If the transaction performs poorly, common equity may lose its entire investment.

Example: financing a $20 million commercial property

Consider a developer acquiring and improving a commercial property with a total project cost of $20 million.

Capital source Amount Share
Senior loan $13,000,000 65%
Mezzanine loan $3,000,000 15%
Preferred equity $2,000,000 10%
Common equity $2,000,000 10%
Total $20,000,000 100%

This structure illustrates how several financing sources can work together to complete a transaction.

But the structure must be tested carefully.

The combined senior and mezzanine debt totals $16 million, or 80% of project cost. That leverage may be unacceptable to some lenders or unsuitable for a property with uncertain cash flow.

A financing structure that works for a stabilized industrial property may be inappropriate for a speculative development project.

Why the capital stack matters

The capital stack influences three fundamental financial outcomes.

Cost of capital. Different financing sources require different returns. A higher concentration of expensive subordinate financing may increase the transaction's overall cost.

Ownership and control. Equity investors may require voting rights, board representation, approval rights, or other forms of influence over major business decisions.

Financial resilience. Debt creates contractual payment obligations. Equity can provide additional flexibility but may dilute existing ownership.

The lowest-cost financing arrangement is not necessarily the most appropriate. A structure must also account for cash flow, repayment capacity, business objectives, and downside scenarios.

Building the right capital structure

Before selecting financing sources, businesses should evaluate their projected cash flow, available collateral, existing obligations, ownership objectives, and anticipated exit strategy.

It is equally important to evaluate how the financing arrangement performs if revenues decline, interest rates rise, or a planned sale or refinancing is delayed.

At The Medley Group, we believe capital structuring should begin with the business objective — not simply the amount of financing requested.

The right capital is not just capital that gets a transaction closed. It is capital structured to support the business after closing.
