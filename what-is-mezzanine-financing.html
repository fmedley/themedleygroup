What Is Mezzanine Financing?

How businesses bridge the gap between senior debt and equity — and when the extra leverage is worth it.

Fred Medley·CEO, The Medley Group·6 min read·November 2026

Businesses pursuing major acquisitions, expansion projects, or commercial real estate developments often encounter a common challenge: traditional lenders are unwilling to finance the entire transaction.

The company may have a strong business model, valuable assets, and significant growth opportunities, yet still face a financing shortfall.

One potential solution is mezzanine financing — a form of subordinated capital that combines characteristics of traditional debt with certain equity-like features.

It can provide businesses with additional financing capacity while potentially allowing existing owners to retain a larger ownership interest.

How mezzanine financing works

A mezzanine lender provides financing that ranks behind senior debt in the agreed repayment hierarchy.

Because mezzanine lenders accept greater risk, they generally require higher returns than senior lenders.

Compensation may include cash interest, payment-in-kind interest, origination fees, and, in some transactions, warrants or other equity participation rights.

The exact structure varies depending on whether the financing supports a corporate acquisition, an operating business, or a commercial real estate investment.

Example: a $30 million business acquisition

Assume an established company plans to acquire a competitor for $30 million. Its proposed financing arrangement is:

Financing source Amount
Senior acquisition loan $18,000,000
Buyer equity $7,000,000
Financing shortfall $5,000,000
Total required $30,000,000

Without additional financing, the buyer would need to contribute another $5 million, find an equity investor, negotiate seller financing, or restructure the transaction.

A mezzanine lender might consider providing the additional $5 million, subject to underwriting and the senior lender's approval.

The resulting capital structure would be $18 million in senior debt, $5 million in mezzanine financing, and $7 million in buyer equity — financing 76.7% of the acquisition through debt.

That leverage creates opportunity, but it also increases financial risk.

Understanding the cost of mezzanine capital

Consider a hypothetical $5 million mezzanine loan carrying 12% annual cash interest.

Illustrative annual interest expense
$600,000
$5,000,000 × 12% = $600,000 per year

This example excludes principal repayments, fees, equity participation, and other financing expenses. The 12% rate is hypothetical, not a current market quote.

The borrower must determine whether the expected incremental earnings and strategic benefits justify this additional expense.

Importantly, the interest rate alone does not establish the financing's total economic cost. A mezzanine arrangement containing warrants, exit fees, or payment-in-kind interest can be considerably more expensive than its stated cash coupon suggests.

Why businesses use mezzanine financing

Mezzanine financing can be particularly useful in several circumstances.

Acquisitions. A buyer may use mezzanine capital to complete a transaction without raising substantial additional common equity.

Growth and expansion. An established company may require financing to enter new markets, expand production capacity, or acquire equipment and operating assets.

Management buyouts. Existing management teams may use subordinated financing to help acquire ownership of the company they operate.

Commercial real estate development. Developers may use mezzanine capital to supplement senior construction or acquisition financing.

Recapitalizations. Business owners may use structured financing to reorganize existing obligations or fund an ownership transition.

The advantages and disadvantages

Potential advantages Important risks
May reduce immediate common equity requirements Higher financing cost
Can support larger transactions Increased leverage
May preserve ownership Possible equity dilution through warrants
Flexible negotiated structures Restrictive covenants and remedies
Can complement senior financing Refinancing and maturity risk

Mezzanine financing should not be treated as a substitute for sound operating cash flow. The additional leverage must be supported by a credible repayment strategy.

Mezzanine financing versus preferred equity

Although the two instruments can serve similar financing objectives, their legal and economic structures differ.

Mezzanine debt generally creates a contractual repayment obligation. Preferred equity represents an ownership investment with negotiated priority and distribution rights.

Both may include complex provisions concerning control, defaults, distributions, and exit events. The choice between them should be based on the entire financing structure rather than a simple comparison of headline returns.

When mezzanine financing makes sense

Mezzanine financing is generally more appropriate when a business has a credible operating history, sustainable cash flow, and a well-defined strategy for repayment or refinancing.

It may be less appropriate for companies with unstable earnings, excessive existing leverage, or highly speculative growth projections.

Before pursuing mezzanine capital, business owners should ask: Can the business comfortably service all debt obligations? What happens if projected earnings decline? Does the financing include equity participation? What rights does the mezzanine lender receive if the company defaults?

These questions are just as important as the amount of financing available.
