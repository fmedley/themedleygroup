The Gap a Lender Sees First: When the Interest Reserve Runs Out

How the interest reserve, the capital stack, and the permanent-financing exit determine whether an otherwise-sound development actually gets financed.

Fred Medley·Founder & CEO, The Medley Group·9 min read·November 2026

Picture a $40 million ground-up multifamily development approaching what was supposed to be the final stage of lease-up. Construction is substantially complete, residents are moving in, and the developer is preparing for permanent financing. By most measures, the project appears to be moving in the right direction.

But beneath the surface, a problem is developing.

The interest reserve — the capital allocated to cover interest payments during construction and lease-up — is nearly exhausted. Rental absorption is running behind projections, operating income has not reached anticipated levels, and the property is not yet generating sufficient cash flow to service its debt independently.

The sponsor now faces a difficult decision: contribute additional capital, negotiate an extension with the construction lender, or attempt to secure financing under considerably less favorable circumstances.

The project itself may still be fundamentally sound. The problem is that its capital structure was designed around a timeline the market did not deliver. In my experience arranging commercial construction financing, this is one of the most common ways an otherwise-sound deal gets into trouble — and it is a vulnerability experienced lenders look for long before issuing a term sheet.

What the sponsor submitted

Consider a proposed $40 million ground-up multifamily development financed through a combination of senior construction debt and sponsor equity. The original financing request included:

  • A $30 million senior construction loan, representing 75% loan-to-cost (LTC).
  • $10 million in sponsor equity.
  • A $2.1 million interest reserve, funded within the construction loan.
  • A projected 12-month lease-up following construction.
  • Stabilized annual net operating income (NOI) of approximately $2.9 million.
  • An anticipated refinance into permanent financing upon stabilization.

At an assumed capitalization rate of 5.75%, the projected stabilized value was approximately $50.43 million — a yield on cost of roughly 7.25%, with the stabilized valuation comfortably exceeding total development costs. On paper, the economics appeared attractive. The challenge was not the quality of the real estate or the market opportunity; it was whether the financing structure could withstand a departure from the original assumptions.

The assumption the lender challenged

Rather than focusing on projected rents or construction costs, the lender examined two interconnected risks: the adequacy of the interest reserve and the reliability of the permanent-financing exit.

The reserve was built around a 12-month lease-up. But what if competing developments delivered into the same submarket, concessions increased, or absorption slowed to 18 to 20 months? Under those conditions, the property could exhaust its reserve before generating enough cash flow to support its debt. Using an illustrative 7.00% construction interest rate, the $30 million loan generates roughly $2.1 million in annual interest once fully drawn. Under the downside scenario, the sponsor would need roughly $850,000 in additional capital simply to cover the interest shortfall — before any construction overruns, additional leasing costs, or extension fees.

The lender then turned to the exit. Applying a conservative 7.50% stressed permanent rate, a 30-year amortization, and a 1.25x minimum debt-service coverage ratio (DSCR), a $30 million permanent loan would carry about $2.52 million of annual debt service. Against $2.9 million of NOI, that is a DSCR of approximately 1.15x — below the lender's minimum. Two weaknesses had emerged: the project was vulnerable to an extended lease-up, and the existing debt exceeded what the property could comfortably refinance under stressed assumptions. Neither made the development unfinanceable — but both warranted restructuring the capital stack.

Restructuring the capital stack

The solution was not to defend the original projections. It was to build a structure capable of performing even if they did not. The revised capital stack made five adjustments:

  • Reduce the senior construction loan from $30 million to $26 million.
  • Introduce a $1 million subordinate mezzanine tranche, subject to senior-lender approval and acceptable intercreditor terms.
  • Increase sponsor equity from $10 million to $14 million.
  • Expand the interest reserve from $2.1 million to $3 million.
  • Add an interest-rate cap (roughly $100,000) on the floating-rate construction loan.

The larger reserve and rate cap raised the total development budget from $40 million to $41 million. More importantly, the restructuring reduced the project's combined debt from $30 million to $27 million — the senior LTC fell from 75% to about 63.4%, and combined leverage (including mezzanine) to about 65.9%. This was not merely a redistribution of risk among lenders; it was a meaningful reduction in overall leverage.

Strengthening the permanent-financing exit

An adequate reserve addresses only one side of construction-lending risk. The other is a credible path to permanent financing. With $2.9 million of stabilized NOI at a 5.75% cap rate, the development supports an estimated value of about $50.43 million. The revised structure targets a $27.5 million permanent loan — roughly 54.5% loan-to-value.

At an illustrative 6.00% permanent rate over 30 years, annual debt service is about $1.98 million, a DSCR of roughly 1.47x. Even stressed to 7.50%, debt service of about $2.31 million produces a DSCR of approximately 1.26x — above the 1.25x minimum, though with limited cushion.

The cost of a stronger structure

There is, of course, a trade-off. The revised structure requires an additional $4 million of equity, mezzanine capital costs more than senior debt, and rate protection is not free. These can affect the sponsor's projected return and internal rate of return.

But the economics of a financing should not be judged solely on the amount of leverage obtained or the lowest available rate. A structure that maximizes leverage yet leaves little room for delays, rising rates, or slow absorption can become far more expensive than a conservatively structured alternative — through emergency capital calls, distressed refinancing, extension fees, dilution, or, in extreme cases, the loss of sponsor equity.

The least expensive capital at closing is not always the least expensive capital over the life of a project.

What experienced lenders are really looking for

Experienced lenders recognize that development rarely unfolds exactly as projected. Schedules change, rates move, competing properties deliver, concessions rise, expenses run over, and capital markets tighten. The question is not whether a developer can produce a compelling pro forma under favorable assumptions — most can. It is whether the development remains viable when those assumptions are challenged. A well-structured request should demonstrate:

  • Adequate capitalization to withstand construction and lease-up delays.
  • An interest reserve supported by detailed monthly cash-flow projections.
  • A sustainable balance among senior debt, subordinate capital, and equity.
  • A credible permanent-financing exit under conservative rate assumptions.
  • Sensitivity analysis across NOI, capitalization rates, and borrowing costs.
  • Sufficient sponsor liquidity and contingency planning.

The takeaway for developers

The gap in this hypothetical was never the real estate. It was a capital structure that depended too heavily on the original timeline and favorable refinancing conditions. A developer can have an exceptional location, an experienced team, and attractive projected returns and still hit financing trouble because the structure lacks flexibility.

Before submitting a financing request, ask the questions a lender will ask first:

  • What happens if construction takes longer than anticipated?
  • What happens if lease-up extends another six to eight months?
  • What happens if permanent rates are 150 basis points higher than projected?
  • What happens if stabilized NOI falls below expectations?

And most importantly: does the capital structure provide enough flexibility to navigate those circumstances without jeopardizing the investment? These questions should shape the financing strategy from the start, not surface first during a lender's underwriting.

The best time to find the gap is before the lender does.

Capital is more than financing. It's strategy.
