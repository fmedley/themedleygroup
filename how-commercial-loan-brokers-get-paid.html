How Commercial Loan Brokers Get Paid

Fees, commissions, transparency, and what every borrower should understand before signing an engagement.

Fred Medley·CEO, The Medley Group·7 min read·November 2026

Securing commercial financing can be complicated.

Banks, private lenders, institutional investors, and alternative financing providers often have different underwriting standards, collateral requirements, lending limits, and approval processes.

Commercial loan brokers help businesses navigate these options by identifying potential financing sources and facilitating transactions.

But one question every borrower should ask before engaging a broker is: How does the broker get paid?

Understanding the compensation arrangement is essential to evaluating the total cost of financing and identifying potential conflicts of interest.

What does a commercial loan broker do?

A commercial loan broker acts as an intermediary between a business seeking financing and potential lenders.

Depending on the engagement, the broker's responsibilities may include evaluating financing requirements, reviewing financial information, identifying lenders, coordinating applications, comparing proposed terms, and assisting with the closing process.

Some firms operate primarily as referral sources, while others provide more comprehensive capital advisory services. The scope of work matters because it influences the compensation structure.

Five common compensation arrangements

1. Success fees

A success fee is compensation earned when a financing transaction closes or another specifically defined milestone is achieved. The fee is often calculated as a percentage of the funded loan amount.

For example, a broker charging a hypothetical 1.5% success fee on a $10 million commercial loan would receive $150,000.

The borrower should understand whether the fee is calculated on the approved amount, committed amount, or actual funded proceeds.

2. Lender-paid referral fees

Some commercial lenders compensate brokers for introducing qualified borrowers or facilitating completed transactions. Under this arrangement, the lender pays the broker directly.

However, lender-paid compensation should not automatically be interpreted as free financing for the borrower. Lenders may account for acquisition costs in their overall pricing.

3. Upfront engagement or advisory fees

Certain financing engagements require substantial work before a lender can evaluate the transaction.

A broker or advisor may charge an upfront fee for defined services, such as preparing a financing package, analyzing financial statements, or developing a capital strategy.

Borrowers should distinguish a legitimate advisory engagement from an unsupported promise to obtain financing. An upfront payment does not guarantee loan approval.

4. Retainer agreements

Complex financing transactions may involve a monthly or fixed retainer. This structure can be appropriate when an advisor provides ongoing services, coordinates multiple financing sources, or works on a transaction over an extended period.

The agreement should specify the services provided, the payment schedule, and whether the retainer is credited against a future success fee.

5. Combined compensation arrangements

Some engagements involve a combination of retainers, advisory fees, and closing-based compensation. These arrangements require particular attention to disclosure, termination rights, and possible conflicts of interest.

How much could a commercial loan broker earn?

There is no single universal commission rate across commercial financing. Fees depend on the transaction size, complexity, financing product, services performed, contractual arrangements, and applicable regulations.

The following table demonstrates how different hypothetical fee percentages affect the cost of a transaction.

Loan amount 0.5% fee 1.0% fee 2.0% fee
$1 million $5,000 $10,000 $20,000
$5 million $25,000 $50,000 $100,000
$10 million $50,000 $100,000 $200,000
$30 million $150,000 $300,000 $600,000

These are mathematical examples, not verified market-average commission rates or recommended fee schedules. Actual compensation may differ substantially.

A larger transaction does not necessarily justify a proportionately larger fee. Some engagements use negotiated flat fees or declining percentage schedules.

Who pays the broker?

Depending on the transaction, compensation may come from the borrower, lender, or another contractually permitted party.

Borrowers should request written disclosure identifying the compensation recipient, the amount or calculation method, the party responsible for payment, and the circumstances that trigger the fee.

A borrower should also ask whether the broker receives additional compensation from any financing provider being recommended. Transparency helps the borrower evaluate whether a proposed financing arrangement serves its interests.

Important regulatory considerations

Commercial financing compensation is subject to a complex regulatory environment.

Unlike many consumer mortgage transactions, commercial financing does not operate under one uniform nationwide broker compensation framework. State licensing and disclosure requirements may apply depending on the financing product, transaction amount, location, and activities performed.

Recent years have seen growing regulatory attention to commercial financing disclosure, with a number of states enacting commercial financing disclosure laws and California expanding its commercial financing licensing requirements.

SBA financing involves additional considerations. Federal regulations govern certain agent compensation arrangements, including required compensation agreements and restrictions on receiving payment from multiple parties for specified activities.

Compensation for arranging equity securities can also trigger federal and state broker-dealer registration issues. A commercial loan broker should not assume that its authority to arrange debt financing automatically permits it to receive transaction-based compensation for securities placements.

Seven questions borrowers should ask

Before signing a commercial financing agreement, borrowers should understand:

  1. Who pays the broker, and how is compensation calculated?
  2. Is the fee earned only when the financing closes?
  3. Are any upfront payments refundable?
  4. Does the broker receive compensation from the lender?
  5. Is the engagement exclusive?
  6. What happens if the borrower secures financing independently?
  7. Are all fees and potential conflicts disclosed in writing?

These questions can help prevent unexpected costs and misunderstandings.

The difference between a loan broker and a capital advisor

Although the terms are sometimes used interchangeably, the services can differ considerably.

A loan broker may focus on identifying a lender willing to finance a specific request. A capital advisor may examine the broader transaction, including the borrower's balance sheet, financing alternatives, capital structure, debt capacity, and long-term financial objectives.

For example, a business requesting a $15 million loan may ultimately benefit from a different combination of senior debt, subordinated financing, or equity capital. The goal should be to identify a suitable financing solution — not simply to complete a transaction.
